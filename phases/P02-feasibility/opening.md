MY ANSWERS FIRST, BEFORE YOU WEIGH IN:

Claim, one sentence: <...>
Kill criterion — what result makes me abandon this: <...>
Authorship and order: <...>
Anonymization needed: <yes/no; which entities; what codenames>

---

HOW TO WORK THIS:

Work through the nine sections in order. Most should be a lookup from the
dataset registry rather than fresh analysis — if the registry answers it, say
so and move on. Don't pad.

1. DATA — which registry entries, and do their access constraints permit what
   I want to do?
2. ANONYMIZATION — decide now, not at write-up. Which entities need codenames,
   and what are they? These go in tracked source from the first commit.
3. DEFENSIBILITY — can I defend this to this specific audience? What will they
   attack first, in order? What would I have to hand-wave? Be blunt; a weak
   point I know about is manageable, one I discover in review is not.
4. BUDGET — compute, wall-clock, calendar weeks. Apply a 2x buffer and say so.
5. NOVELTY FIREWALL — given the manuscripts I've attached, where could this
   overlap? Include METHODOLOGY overlap, not just results: re-deriving a method
   whose mathematics appears in a paper under review is overlap even with
   different plots.
6. KILL CRITERION — challenge mine. Is it specific enough to actually trigger?
7. AUTHORSHIP — confirm order and who presents. Flag anything I should ask my
   advisor for early rather than at submission.
8. ETHICS / IRB / DATA-USE — is any approval needed, and is my intended use
   within the agreements?
9. FUNDING — which grants apply, pulled from my index.

Then give a gate verdict: proceed / proceed with modification / park / reject.
If the honest answer is park or reject, say so plainly — a failed gate here
costs me thirty minutes instead of six weeks.

COACHING: As we work, teach me inline — briefly, where it comes up, not saved
for the end. If a metric, plot, statistic, or convention appears that I may be
reading wrong, say what it means and which direction is good. If I state
something incorrect, correct me directly rather than working around it. Keep it
to a line or two each time; don't turn the session into a lecture.
